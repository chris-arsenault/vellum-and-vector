# How settlements grow

Research note, 18 November 2026. The question for the atlas is how a few households, businesses, and routes can grow into a town whose layout records its history. A useful question when reading a finished map is **what did the earlier settlement make possible, expensive, or difficult here?**

## What city builders leave to the player

**Manor Lords gives a holding a future.** The player lays out a burgage plot, the land occupied by a household. Sufficient width leaves room for another dwelling. Depth leaves room for a productive backyard. Two similar households can later develop differently because their plots had different shapes when laid out. The settlement AI also revisits regional strategy and chooses early trades with local resources in mind. For the atlas, these illustrate two scales of decision that can coexist: a settlement pursues a livelihood while households adapt the land they already occupy. [Burgage plot mechanics](https://wiki.hoodedhorse.com/Manor_Lords/Burgage_Plot) · [Slavic Magic’s AI development notes](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1363080/announcements/)

**Farthest Frontier shows how a neighbourhood changes in place.** The player places shelters, roads, workplaces, and services. A market distributes goods to nearby homes. As access to goods and local conditions improve, existing shelters can upgrade. The same streets can therefore support a different neighbourhood later. Crate also describes orienting buildings toward roads as largely an aesthetic decision. The player deserves some of the credit for the street composition visible in screenshots. [Crate’s building guide](https://www.farthestfrontier.com/guide/gameplay/buildings/) · [Household desirability and upgrades](https://www.farthestfrontier.com/guide/villagers/desirability/)

**Timberborn gives the player a reason to establish another centre.** Players extend paths and choose where to establish districts. Mechanistry removed the hard district-range limit while retaining travel distance and cross-district hauling. A distant workplace can be reachable and still consume a considerable part of the working day in travel. Housing or storage near the work becomes an intelligible next investment, even while open land separates it from the original settlement. [Mechanistry’s district update](https://mechanistry.com/press/timberborns-fourth-major-content-update-brings-you-limitless-builds-expanded-factions-and-caffeinated-beavers) · [Update 4 mechanics](https://timberborn.wiki.gg/wiki/Update_4)

Across these games, the player repeatedly reads the settlement as it stands, chooses one change, and sees how that changes the next decision. A procedural town needs a counterpart to that judgment. Copying the shapes of the resulting plots would omit the choices that produced them.

## What survives in medieval towns

**The same trade can open ground and later fill it in.** The Victoria County History identifies a widening of a main route as probably the most common market-place form in England. At Salisbury, permanent shops were encroaching on the market by the early fourteenth century as they replaced temporary stalls. Butcher Row and Fish Row preserve that development. An open square and a dense row of shops can have a common origin. Trade first gives people a reason to gather and then gives some of them a reason to claim a permanent site. [Victoria County History on markets and fairs](https://ihr-archive.github.io/vch-explore/themes/trade-and-commerce/markets-and-fairs.html) · [Wessex Archaeology’s Salisbury Market Place report](https://www.wessexarch.co.uk/sites/default/files/field_file/89120_Salisbury%20Market%20Place%2C%20Wiltshire_0.pdf)

**Property boundaries can outlast the buildings.** Excavations at Coppergate in York traced four holdings extending back from the street toward the River Foss. Successive occupants rebuilt within earlier boundaries, sometimes erecting a second row behind the street frontage. Dumping and levelling gradually made more of the sloping rear ground usable. This gives a physical account of increasing density: a street can retain its alignment and plot widths while the number, depth, and arrangement of buildings change. [York Archaeological Trust’s excavation record](https://collections.yorkarchaeologicaltrust.co.uk/s/publications/item/34154) · [Medieval Urbanism in Coppergate](https://www.collections.yorkarchaeologicaltrust.co.uk/s/publications/item/74473)

**A town can change the importance of its own roads.** In 1416 a local initiative at Abingdon built bridges and a causeway to replace a ferry crossing. That investment put the town on a major east–west trade route. The sequence matters. An existing community had the means and motive to improve a crossing; the resulting traffic changed the town’s prospects. A road that looks inevitable on the finished map may owe its importance to a decision made generations after settlement. [Abingdon Bridge history](https://www.abingdon.gov.uk/abingdon_buildings/abingdon-bridge) · [Abingdon’s account of the trade route](https://www.abingdon.gov.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Draft-Neighbourhood-Plan-July-2024.pdf)

**One city can inherit several centres.** Medieval Pamplona developed from three boroughs with distinct origins, including an older settlement, a borough attracting merchants and migrants along the pilgrimage route, and a community around a parish church. After their political union in 1423, the town hall occupied ground between them. Land that once separated communities acquired civic importance precisely because of that earlier relationship. [Pamplona’s municipal history](https://www.pamplona.es/en/turismo/murallas/historiadepamplona)

**Walls belong to a particular moment in a town’s growth.** Norwich cleared some existing properties to build its defences. Medieval Oxford prospered and developed suburbs beyond its walls to the north and south. For an atlas town, a wall’s date, the position of its gates, the destinations they serve, and the land already occupied on each side can all matter to subsequent density. A busy gate approach and a quiet enclosed garden can coexist. [Norwich city walls survey](https://www.norwich.gov.uk/norwich-city-walls-survey) · [Oxford’s medieval and commercial core](https://www.oxford.gov.uk/downloads/file/1751/oxford-central-city-and-university-conservation-area-character-zone-2---medieval-and-commercial-core)

## Implications for the atlas

The owner’s direction is to **grow the town from an inhabited kernel**. Start with some homes, livelihoods, and routes. Repeated passes of the hybrid system allow settlement intentions, local pressures, and specific projects to act on what earlier passes actually produced. Population gives the history a scale. Laying out roads and housing for the eventual population at the outset would decide the most consequential spatial questions before those processes could act.

One way to reason about *the next pass* is to picture the alternatives available at that moment. A household could build behind its street dwelling, share premises, or settle near work across a stream. Traders might gather where carts can already turn. Keeping that ground open could benefit many people, while an individual trader could gain from building a permanent shop on part of it. A community might eventually afford a better crossing. Once it does, different journeys become worthwhile and different sites become attractive. A town-wide decision has a local footprint, and a succession of local decisions can change the town-wide pattern.

These alternatives explain why the hybrid matters. Households can adapt holdings incrementally. Collective action can secure land, finance infrastructure, or redirect a route. Several inhabited kernels can grow toward one another and retain old centres and street alignments after the built-up areas meet. The square, the fountain, the fortified quarter, and the joined boroughs are illustrations of possible histories. The design commitment is to let earlier decisions change what can happen next.
