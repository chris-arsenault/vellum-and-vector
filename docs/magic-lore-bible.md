# Magic lore bible

Setting-neutral lore for the seven magic systems in
[magic system candidates](magic-system-candidates.md). It sits at the level of
a school of magic, not a setting: each entry says what the system is made of,
where the generator finds it, what it does to rock, weather and living things,
how a people uses it and what that costs, and enough lore to make it feel like
one thing rather than a slot. Peoples, gods, places and events are left to the
world author. "Practitioner" means anyone of any kind who works the system.

The current three are implemented as described in the
[product contract](product-spec.md#103-magical-generation--fire-aurora-and-pressure).
The extensions and the four new systems are design.

## The shape of the seven

Each system has a medium, and acts only through it. Fire is in stone, Aurora in
ice, Pressure in deep water, Greenwood in the water inside living things, Salt
in brine, Veil in the tear between two others, Hallowed in what people keep.
None of them is a fuel for another. Where two meet they interfere, and the
interference is itself geography: a thermal rim, a steam fracture, a front of
dying trees, a tear.

Every system costs its practitioner something that the system is made of. Fire
takes flesh, Aurora takes the boundary of the self, Pressure takes presence,
Greenwood takes rootedness, Salt takes the ability to forget, Veil takes the
certainty of where one is, Hallowed takes the freedom to leave.

## Fire

_The hearth that can be carried._

**Physical character.** Fire is stored energy in mineral form. A fire-core is a
dense stone that holds a charge of heat compressed into it by the body of the
world, and releases it when cracked. Cores form in two ways. A vein core grows
where geothermal heat rises through a fracture network under land; the fracture
is its conduit, and while the conduit is open the vein makes more cores and the
land above it stays warm. A fallen core arrives from outside in an impact; it is
a finite stock scattered as ejecta around a crater and carried downstream by
rivers as fragments in gravel. Fallen cores carry a metal the world did not
make, and a compass misreads near a field of them.

**Where it is found.** Vein Fire is found at geothermal maxima under land, never
under the sea floor, where the same heat shows as vents and belongs to no
system. Its halos follow the fractures, so a Fire country is a branching one.
Fallen Fire is found at craters, in the fan downwind of them and in river
gravels below them, which is where its placer miners work.

**Effects on the world.** Fire heats the ground, melts ice and hardens the rock
it passes through. It quickens living things: growth, turnover, seeding and
decay all run faster around a core, and the warmth travels down the rivers
below it. Its biomes are the Ember grove at the core and the Thaw meadow on the
melt line. A vein opens its conduits as it flows until it has carried its
budget, then seals, and a sealed vein is a cold hollow with a dead town on it.
A crater field is the opposite shape: a lake in a ring, an ejecta fan that
grows nothing for a long time, and placer bands that make a river rich for a
stretch.

**Practice.** Fire practice is hearth-keeping. A core is a hearth in a stone,
and the practitioner's first skill is banking it: opening it so slowly that it
warms a house for a season, or a forge for a day, without going out. The second
skill is the opposite, cracking it at once so that it cuts stone, collapses a
tunnel or unmakes a wall. Both are done by hand, with the core held and struck
and the release taken through the body, and every use burns. A practitioner's
hands are a record: casual use reddens them, a career leaves them mottled and
stiff, the joints locked, the skin unable to close its own cracks. Practitioners
read burns the way others read ledgers. A clean burn is a clean release.

**Lore.** Fire traditions hold that a house is wherever its coals are. When a
people moves, it does not leave its fire behind. The live coals are raked from
the old hearth into a covered pot and carried, and the hearth's keeper is
understood to ride the coals to the new place, where it is greeted with bread
and salt. To abandon a hearth with its fire still in it is the worst luck a
Fire people knows, and a sealed vein is read as exactly that: a house whose
coals no one carried, now cold, with the keeper still in it. A Fire town that
hears its vein is nearing its budget begins choosing which cores to carry.

The traditions also teach that a core's heat is hidden the way a death is
hidden: inside the stone, inside the vein, inside the mountain, each inside the
next, so that whoever would reach it must open every layer. This is why cores
are cracked and not burned. The heat is not on the surface of the stone; it is
nested at the centre, and the venting pick finds the one place where the
nesting can be undone. Fallen cores trouble this teaching. They are coals from a
hearth that burned somewhere else and arrived without a keeper. Some schools
refuse them as strangers' fire. Others carry them as one would carry a
foundling, and a smith who works their metal is known by the colour of the
blade.

**Interference.** Fire and Aurora cancel where they meet, and the line of
cancellation is a thermal rim of cracked ground. Fire released near carried
Pressure is destructive to both. Fire boils Greenwood's springs into steam
fractures and overdrives its growth into rot. Fire burns Salt ground clean.
Fire conduit opening breaks a Hallowed ward line. Fire release ruptures a Veil
door.

## Aurora

_The light that withdrew to leave room, and what it left in the ice._

**Physical character.** Aurora is a charge that falls from the sky toward cold
ground and is kept in ice. Thick ice holds it; thin ice and warm ground let it
drain toward colder neighbours. The charge itself does nothing violent. It keeps.
Ice that holds it does not ablate, the ground near it is a few degrees colder
than it should be, and whatever is inside it slows: growth, decay, turnover, the
ripening of anything. Aurora-crystals are formations in the ice that collect and
hold charge densely enough to be carried, and a practitioner works through one.

**Where it is found.** On cold high ground, on ice caps and glaciers, and in the
cold drainages that lead toward them. The field is strongest where ice is
thickest and oldest. A relict charge stays in ground the ice has left, so a
valley that was glaciated keeps a cold shadow for a long time after the glacier
is gone.

**Effects on the world.** Aurora weakens the rock it sits in over an age. It
keeps ice from melting that climate alone would melt, so Aurora ground is
colder and whiter than its latitude. Its stillness slows the living landscape
into the Rime forest, where trees grow for centuries, and the Glow-lichen
barrens, where almost nothing turns over. What the ice preserved is released
where the field retreats, and a retreating Aurora edge is a line of sudden
decay, a rich strip, the melt line a people settles on.

**Practice.** Aurora is slow magic. A practitioner attunes to a crystal through
sustained attention and draws its charge into an act of perception: reading
what the ice holds of the past, seeing a distant place as it is now, or at the
height of the art, fixing a moment into a construct of ice that replays it.
Nothing about it is quick, and nothing about it harms a body at a distance. The
cost is to the boundary of the self. A reader is a vessel, and charge is light;
a vessel that takes more light than it can hold breaks, and what breaks does not
come back whole. Casual use costs little. Sustained use cracks the reader a
little at a time, and the oldest readers are held together by habit, unsure
which of their memories are their own. Traditions ration their readers with
strict rotations of practice and rest, and retire a reader at the first crack
they can see.

**Lore.** Aurora traditions begin with a withdrawal. The light, they say, was
once everywhere, and nothing else could exist in it; so it drew back into
itself and left a space, and the world is what grew in the space it left. The
cold shadow around Aurora ground is read as the same act repeated small: the
field withdraws warmth from a place so that something else, slower, can be
there. A Rime forest is not a dead place to them. It is a place the light made
room for, and the long lives of its trees are the proof.

Then the breaking. When the light first returned into the space it had left, it
came too strongly for the vessels made to hold it, and they shattered. The
shards fell into the ice, and the light they held fell with them. This is what
an Aurora-crystal is in the tradition's account: a shard of a vessel that
broke, still holding a spark of what broke it. The melt line is where the
sparks come loose, and a reader's work is gathering. Every reading lifts a
spark out of the ice and returns it to where it belonged, and the traditions
hold that when every spark has been gathered the world as it stands will end,
because the space the light left will be full again. No reader expects to see
this. The strictest schools say that a reader who gathers too eagerly is
hurrying the end, and that the rotation of practice and rest is there to slow
the world's completion as much as to protect the reader.

**Interference.** Aurora cancels Fire along a thermal rim. Aurora-crystals
dissipate carried Pressure. Aurora stillness freezes a Greenwood front. Aurora
over Salt ground is undeath: what Salt kept, Aurora will not let decay, and the
dead do not rest. Aurora reinforces a Hallowed ward and closes a Veil door.

## Pressure

_The river too strong to land unbroken._

**Physical character.** Pressure is a force that originates at a locus in the
deep ocean and travels outward through water as rays. Rays reflect from coasts
and shelves, cross and reinforce one another, and where they shoal they lift
deep water. A shoaling ray makes an upwelling band of cold water at the surface
and fogs the coast it faces. Behind a strong front the water is left warm and
still, a wake. Where many rays meet, the sea crushes: a crush zone grinds the
bottom and the shore, and its history is a record of wakes and rogue impacts. A
front large enough changes the climate of the coast it reaches, and the storm
that rises from a warm wake under a cold sky is the front continued onto land.

**Where it is found.** In the deep, and wherever the deep's rays reach: coasts,
shelves, straits, the lee of islands, and inland along storm tracks. A coast's
Pressure character depends on its angle to the locus and what lies between.

**Effects on the world.** Pressure stresses coasts and shapes them. Its upwelling
feeds fisheries and cools the sea surface; its fog makes the Fog heath and the
Moss forest on the coasts facing a band; its wakes warm the water behind them.
Its storms carry rain and snow onto windward slopes, scour soil, drive dunes
and leave a dry lee, so a weather-wall range with a desert behind it is a
Pressure signature as surely as a fogbound coast.

**Practice.** The practitioner descends. The deeper the descent, the more
weight is available, and at sufficient depth the weight becomes a carrier for
intent. The practitioner opens to it, is filled, and surfaces carrying a charge.
With it they speak across distances through vibration too low to hear, exert
force without contact and, in the oldest practice, commune with whatever
answers from the deep. Depth tolerance limits who can practise, and the limit is
physical, not cultural; a people that cannot dive cannot do this. On land, the
same tradition reads storms: a sky-reader knows which wake will raise weather
and where the track will run. The cost is distance. What is carried up wanders
inside the carrier for a long time before it finds a way out, and a practitioner
who has carried much is present but lagged, answering after a long pause, always
partly somewhere deep.

**Lore.** Pressure traditions tell that the deep was brought down for the sake
of the dead. Someone owed rites to ancestors who could not be reached, and
asked for the deep to come where they were; and the deep answered that its fall
would break whatever it landed on. So it was caught. The teaching is that a
force this large can never arrive directly. It must be received in something
that breaks its fall and braids it into streams, and the coasts of the world are
that receiver. A shelf, a reef, a scatter of islands catches the ray and splits
it; the fog and the fisheries and the warm wakes are the braided streams, and a
coast with nothing to catch the fall takes it whole and is ground to a crush
zone. Sky-readers say that a storm is one of the streams reaching inland, still
looking for the ground it was owed.

The same story explains the practitioner. What is caught in a receiver wanders
there a long time. The deep, once caught, circled for ages before it was let
down, and the charge a practitioner carries up does the same inside them,
circling until it is released through speech too low to hear or force without a
hand. The traditions also tell of the one who, angered by the river's flood,
swallowed it whole and had to let it out again through his ears. They use this
to teach restraint. A practitioner who takes in more than they can release is
not a vessel but a swallower, and what comes out of a swallower comes out
wrong. The cost of distance is the wandering: a part of the carrier is always
in the receiver, circling, and each descent adds to it.

**Interference.** Carried Pressure dissipates near Aurora-crystals. Pressure
and Fire destroy each other on contact, and a Pressure/Aurora breach is a
coast broken by both. Fire heat columns feed a Pressure storm; Aurora's cold
shadow pins a front in place; a Hallowed rim turns a storm track. Pressure
upwelling salts a Greenwood spring on the coast. Carried Pressure cannot pass a
Veil door.

## Greenwood

_The seed is the same everywhere. The ground decides._

**Physical character.** Greenwood is a vitality carried in sap and groundwater.
It moves root to root through connected living cover and along aquifers under
the ground, and surfaces where an aquifer reaches air as a spring. It does not
cross bare rock, open water or ice. Where it surfaces without cover it makes an
oasis, and the oasis grows cover, so a spring and a forest are the same system
meeting itself. Its two origins are the oldest stands of high-biomass cover and
deep peat, and the outcrops where an aquifer breaks the surface.

**Where it is found.** In old-growth forest, in deep peatland, along spring
lines at the foot of karst and at the edges of basins, in oasis chains across
dry ground. A Greenwood country is recognisable from the air as forest where
the climate says there should be none.

**Effects on the world.** Greenwood holds forest beyond its climate band,
deepens soil, braids rivers into wetland and regrows cleared land within an
epoch. Its biomes are the Deepwood, the Wending fen and the Well meadow around
a spring. The water at a spring heals and fertilises. Drawn too hard, the karst
beneath a spring collapses into sinkholes, and a well town that overdraws its
spring sits on a field of them.

**Practice.** Greenwood practice is soil-making. The practitioner cannot change
what Greenwood is or how far it reaches; they can only change the ground it
reaches into. They deepen soil, open and close channels, graft, plant, mark
what must not be cut and clear what crowds. A healer works at a spring, and
healing is the water's act with the practitioner as its hand. Nothing about it
is fast; a grove planted on bare ground is a century from being real, and the
one who plants it will not see it. The cost is rootedness. A practitioner
becomes part of the ground they have made. They sicken when it is cut, cannot
easily leave the country it covers, and the oldest of them do not leave it at
all.

**Lore.** Greenwood traditions teach with four grounds. The vitality falls
alike on all of them, they say, the way a sower casts without choosing; what
differs is the ground. On the trodden path it never enters, and the road, the
bare rock and the ice are the path: Greenwood does not cross them. On thin
soil over stone it enters at once and flares, green in a season, and dies in
the next because there is no depth to hold it; the traditions point to a Fire
halo as the clearest case, where quickening makes the vitality flare and rot.
Among thorns it enters and is choked by what was already growing, and the front
where Greenwood meets Salt is thorn ground, a line of trees that took and were
crowded out. And on deep ground it takes and holds and yields, thirty, sixty, a
hundredfold, and that is the Deepwood.

From this they draw their whole ethic. Since the seed is the same everywhere,
there is no such thing as bad seed, only ground not yet made. A Greenwood people
does not ask whether a place can carry forest; it asks how long the soil will
take. The grove it plants is as real in a hundred years as the primeval one,
and the tender who plants it is understood to be making ground for someone they
will never meet. The traditions end every teaching with the same word, which is
_listen_, and they mean it of the ground: a tender who cannot hear whether a
place is path, stone, thorn or depth cannot work, and the hearing is most of the
training.

**Interference.** Fire quickening overdrives Greenwood into rot and boils its
springs into steam fractures. Aurora stillness freezes its front. Salt kills
Greenwood from below wherever the two meet, and Greenwood buries Salt under
peat where it wins; the line between them is a front of dying trees. Pressure
upwelling salts a coastal spring.

## Salt

_The garden where the water is shown by its absence._

**Physical character.** Salt is a residue in brine. It is what remains where
life ended in numbers or where water died in a closed basin, and it keeps what
it touches from changing. It seeps with groundwater downslope and is retained
where the ground is dry or anoxic; where it reaches a basin it crusts, and the
dry wind carries the crust on as dust. Its two origins are mass death read
from history, the battlefields and sieges and famine years of an era, and dead
water, the endorheic basins and playas where a lake dried.

**Where it is found.** On old battlefields and the ground downstream of them,
around dead lakes, in the dry interiors of continents, in the salt pans of the
rain shadow. Salt ground is mostly made by history, so a young world has little
of it and an old one has a great deal.

**Effects on the world.** Cover thins on Salt ground, grazers avoid it and the
soil sours. From each basin a desiccation front spreads, and the flats at the
centre turn to glass. The biomes are the Barrow heath on old killing ground,
the Salt pan and the Glass flat. Mirage is Salt's perception face: what stands
on a glass flat is seen from far away, and so are things that no longer stand
there.

**Practice.** Salt practice is raking. The practitioner keeps the pan: smooths
the crust, draws the lines on it, sets the stones that mark the dead and
tends the barrows the way others tend fields. The work is done daily and is
never finished, because wind and seep undo it, and the undoing is part of the
practice. From the kept pan a practitioner reads what the ground holds, lays
the dead so that they stay laid, binds an oath in salt so that it cannot be
broken, and preserves what must not decay. The strongest practice raises what
the ground kept, and the traditions that allow it treat it as a debt that comes
due. The cost is the ability to forget. Salt keeps, and the raker is kept too:
every grief stays fresh, every oath binding, every face in the salt still seen.
The old practitioners are not mad. They are complete, and cannot put anything
down.

**Lore.** Salt traditions say that a pan is a garden in which the water is
shown by not being there. The lines a raker draws across the crust are the
ripples of a lake that is gone; the stones set in it are islands in that lake;
and the whole art is to make the absence visible, so that whoever looks at a
Glass flat sees the water that was and the lives that were in it. A dead lake
is not a failure to them. It is the clearest statement a landscape can make,
and the raking keeps the statement legible.

They add a rule that every raker learns first: from wherever you stand, one
stone is hidden. A pan is laid so that no viewpoint shows all its dead at once,
and the traditions hold that this is true of every Salt ground whether or not a
raker laid it. There is always one you cannot see from here. Mirage is read as
the hidden stone showing itself, and the pillars that stand on some flats are
read as those who went looking for it. From the hidden stone comes the ethic of
the oath: a covenant sealed in salt is kept toward the one you cannot see, and
a Salt people's courts and its graveyards are the same institution because both
are places where the absent are still counted present. A city sown with salt
stays sown for the same reason. The sowing made its absence permanent, and the
Barrow heath downstream of an ancient siege is its garden.

**Interference.** Aurora stillness over Salt ground is undeath: the dead that
Salt kept, Aurora will not let decay, and they do not rest. Fire burns Salt
ground clean, and a Fire people will burn a battlefield for that reason.
Greenwood and Salt fight a front wherever they meet. Hallowed halts Salt at its
boundary.

## Veil

_The post the crossing comes down._

**Physical character.** Veil has no source of its own. It is what happens where
two other systems interfere hard enough: a Fire halo against an Aurora field, a
Pressure front against an Aurora coast, a Greenwood front against Salt. Where
the gradients are steep the world tears. A small or shifting tear is a wild
zone, ground whose terrain and cover change between one survey and the next
with no cause a surveyor can name. A large and stable tear pairs with another
stable tear elsewhere, and the pair is a door: a connection between two
located places that are not adjacent.

**Where it is found.** On the rims and fronts between systems, so Veil is found
wherever two of the others meet strongly, and nowhere else. A world with one
magic system has no Veil. A world with many has a great deal, and a world in
crisis, where the fronts are pressing hardest, has the most.

**Effects on the world.** In a wild zone the geography is unreliable: a hill is
not where it was, a stream has changed its bed without a flood, a forest has a
different edge. The atlas marks such ground and shows features in their
alternate positions. A door shows little locally, a ring of odd cover and a cold
pool, and changes everything at the scale of a continent: a route through it
is short, and trade geography stops obeying distance. An inland city with a
door behaves like a port.

**Practice.** Veil is not worked; it is housed. A tear left alone is a wild
zone. A tear that a people builds around becomes a door, and the building is
the practice: a house raised over the tear, and at its centre a post, a whole
trunk or a standing stone or a living tree, which is the axis the crossing
travels. Everything in the house turns around the post. A door-keeper's work is
to keep the house, keep the post, and open the crossing in order, saluting
first what must be saluted first before anything else is asked. A door forced
without a house is a wild zone made worse, and the traditions agree that
forcing one kills. The cost is place. One who crosses is inhabited by the
crossing for a time, and is for that time partly where they left; the oldest
keepers, who have been inhabited many times, speak of their own country as one
of several they might be standing in.

**Lore.** Veil traditions teach that a crossing does not pass through a hole.
It comes down a post. Where two forces press on each other hard enough the
world opens at a point, and what opens there is vertical: an axis between
places, not a gap in a wall. Left bare, the axis has nothing to come down, and
so it comes down into the ground itself, and the ground is ridden. That is a
wild zone in their account: a landscape inhabited by the crossing, moving its
hills and streams as a ridden body moves, until the crossing leaves it and it
settles somewhere not quite where it was. The traditions regard a wild zone with
pity more than fear. It is a door no one built a house for.

A housed door is the opposite. The post gives the crossing a way down that is
not the ground, and the house gives it an order: who is saluted first, who
opens, who may be inhabited and for how long. The keepers say the first
salutation is everything. A crossing opened without greeting the one who keeps
the gate opens onto the wrong place, and the fortress at a door exists less to
stop armies than to make sure the order is kept. The lands on the far side of a
door are spoken of as a neighbouring house with its own post and its own order,
and a door-empire is a people that has learned the orders of many houses.

**Interference.** Veil is made of interference and has none of its own. Aurora
crystals close a door, Fire release ruptures one, carried Pressure cannot pass
through, a Hallowed ward seals one. Every new front between any two systems
makes new Veil.

## Hallowed

_The ground on which nothing is harmed._

**Physical character.** Hallowed is the one system made by people. It
accumulates where they gather over long time, strongest at seats held for many
generations and at the destinations of pilgrimage, and it holds along a line
that people bind: a wall, a border, a river bank. It travels along the routes
that people keep walking and is stored at shrines, in crystal and in set stone.
It decays when the shrine is abandoned or the line is no longer walked. Inside
it the other systems are damped: a Fire halo cools, Salt halts at the
boundary, a Veil door seals, a storm track turns. Along a bound line, geography
stops moving: the river keeps its bed, the coast stops eroding, and a border
becomes a physical discontinuity that can be seen from the air. Whatever
Hallowed damps accumulates at its rim.

**Where it is found.** At old seats and sanctuaries, along pilgrimage roads,
along maintained walls and borders, and in the ruins of all of these, which hold
a fading halo for a long time after the keeping stopped. A Hallowed map is a map
of where people have been restrained.

**Effects on the world.** Hallowed ground is calm ground. Rivers inside it are
stable, coasts inside it hold, the magic of other systems is muted, and crops
and people are safe from the hazards that surround them. The rim is the
opposite: whatever Hallowed held back is piled there, and the country just
outside a great sanctuary is often the most dangerous in the region. There are
no Hallowed biomes. Its signature is that the biomes inside it stay what they
were.

**Practice.** Hallowed practice is restraint. Inside the bound, the earth is
not dug, fire is neither kindled nor put out, water is not dammed or drawn
beyond need, the air is not stirred, and what grows is not cut. The vow has two
grades. Specialists keep it whole: they go on foot, sweep the path before them,
cover the mouth when they speak, and own nothing that would need the ground
opened. Everyone else keeps it in part, minimising what cannot be avoided, and
the traditions are explicit that a household cannot keep the whole vow and
should not pretend to. Consecration is the vow made over a place; a ward line
is the vow made over a boundary; the set stone marks where the vow begins. The
cost is the freedom to leave. A specialist who keeps the whole vow cannot
ride, dig, kindle or carry, and so cannot travel except slowly and on foot
along kept routes, and a people that has bound its border must walk it forever
or watch it fail.

**Lore.** Hallowed traditions begin by counting what is alive. Earth is alive,
water is alive, fire is alive, air is alive, and every plant, each with one
sense, the sense of touch, and each able to be harmed. From this follows
everything. The other six systems are, in Hallowed teaching, harms: Fire burns
the earth-bodies, Pressure crushes the water-bodies, Salt kills the
plant-bodies, Aurora stills them, Greenwood crowds them, Veil tears the ground
itself. Hallowed is simply the ground on which these harms are not done, and
the damping is not a power but a consequence. Where no one digs, Fire has no
conduit opened for it; where no one dams, Pressure's storm has no stilled water
to feed on; where no one cuts, Salt has nothing dying to take. The sanctuary is
calm because within it nothing is hurt.

The rim follows from the two grades of vow. A household that keeps the vow in
part does its unavoidable harm somewhere, and that somewhere is just outside
the bound. The traditions do not hide this. They teach that every sanctuary has
a shadow of displaced harm around it, that the shadow is the price of the calm,
and that a Hallowed people owes its neighbours for what it exports. The
landmark stone is the gravest matter in their law for the same reason. To move
a boundary is to dig the earth, which is harm; it is also to move the line
where restraint ends and displaced harm begins, onto someone who did not agree
to it. A people that moves a stone has broken the vow twice, and the Hallowed
that was held by the stone goes out of the ground.

**Interference.** Hallowed is the interference system. Fire conduit opening
breaks a ward line. Aurora reinforces one. A Hallowed rim turns a Pressure storm
track and halts a Salt front; a Hallowed ward seals a Veil door. Its own cost
is its rim.

## Adjacent sources

Each system's lore was written against one real-world body of thought chosen
to be near the trope without being the trope, so that the entry would not fall
back on genre defaults. The sources were drawn at random from the pool in
[`lore-pool/pool.txt`](lore-pool/pool.txt), 385 texts, practices and concepts
in 53 tradition families, with [`lore-pool/roll.py`](lore-pool/roll.py). The
script rolled three draws (seeds 338923 to 338925), each giving three candidates
per system with no two systems sharing a family, and one candidate per system
was chosen from across the draws. To reroll:

```
python3 docs/lore-pool/roll.py docs/lore-pool/pool.txt --rolls 3
python3 docs/lore-pool/roll.py docs/lore-pool/pool.txt --seed 338923 --rolls 3
```

The second command reproduces the recorded draw. Nothing from the sources is
setting content; each shaped the structure of its entry's teaching.
