# Major works of the atlas

A major work is a specific destination that people elsewhere would recognize. Its size may come from a single mountain face, miles of connected works, a collection accumulated over centuries, or an institution with an exceptional reach. The settlement template determines how people can establish and develop the town. Major works have their own selection and their own POI rules. A city can have a flotilla without a great shipyard or a great shipyard without a flotilla. ## Major-work types and possible instances

The generator chooses a type such as a library, palace or shipyard. A commission of that type develops its own purpose, premises and arrangement. A library that houses visitors for public instruction needs different rooms and access from one that preserves restricted records. A change of spelling or orientation alone would leave those two libraries functionally identical. Each built landmark receives a local name through the settlement's naming culture. Refinement carries the existing landmark's identity, name and design into the child.

The following research examples describe individual places one might design. Their titles illustrate those examples. They supply neither a list of names to publish nor a single prescribed layout for each type.

| Example instance | Who builds it and where | What changes when it exists |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Library of two records | Archivists settle beside enduring ice and build dry stacks across a cold passage from the readers' chambers. Copies, annotations and conflicting testimony accumulate apart from the original impressions. | A petitioner must reach both repositories to challenge a record. Heat-producing workshops compete for sites away from the ice. A mundane great library can instead hold rival collections united by a copying hall. |
| Palace of audiences | A realm with a resident court assembles lodging, petition courts, service yards and private apartments around successive thresholds. | Ambassadors use the public avenue while cooks, clerks and witnesses sustain an equally busy side gate. A change of dynasty can close the old audience court without erasing its approach. |
| Prison of the last landing | A judicial power maintains an isolated island, shaft or shelf with one controlled transfer and independent delivery access. Its archive and visiting court may stand on shore. | Prisoners, petitioners and provisions follow different routes to the same crossing. The transfer crew can have greater practical power than the governor. A kept Veil door inside would defeat the premise unless explicitly controlled. |
| Mountain of faces | Quarry crews and a patron cut a portrait into a suitable exposed face. Rubble must go somewhere. Terraces, a spoil apron and a viewing ground span several scales. | The city may be built partly from its own monument. A later polity can recut the face; old features survive in fragments above workers' lanes. |
| Fleet cradle | Shipwrights need protected water, a timber route and a shore deep enough to launch hulls. Long slips and a fitting basin grow from a working yard. | A ship can shelter a displaced neighborhood while the fleet occupies the slips. An obstructed fairway can close the whole work even with the sheds intact. |
| Processional lake | Several congregations maintain a lake sanctuary, shore kitchens, boat landings and an open route across water. | An ordinary fishing inlet becomes the assembly ground on festival days. Disputes over private quays affect whether villages across the lake can attend. |
| Stair of floods | Downstream households, cultivators and a water authority sponsor connected catch courts, spillways and public steps on a floodplain. | At low water the basins are gathering grounds. During floods the spill route takes precedence over streets. Upstream sponsors and downstream beneficiaries may disagree about which court to enlarge. |
| Parliament of roots | Successive generations of tenders reserve linked springs and rooting corridors. The gathering chambers lie in clearings and among mature trunks. | Cutting an ordinary-looking feeder grove can damage the meeting place far away. A young town may inherit an ancient grove, or begin a work that its founders never see mature. |
| Bell of the soundings | Divers and coastal observers establish sounding stations across a deep channel, with a shore gallery for receiving reports and keeping charts. | Ships seek news of approaching wakes. Some stations may be physically near one another yet reachable only by descent. A stored Pressure charge cannot be carried home through a Veil shortcut. |
| Court of unforgotten names | Rakers maintain a brine pan, barrow avenues and a witness hall on dry ground. Markers and daily walking routes preserve distinct claims on the open salt. | Families travel to renew oaths and contest debts. A lapse in maintenance changes which testimony can be recovered. The work can dominate a sparsely inhabited basin. |
| House of the distant guest | Keepers and host communities at two functioning Veil doors jointly establish reception courts, recovery lodging and places for exchanging goods that cannot cross. | A traveler can step into another climate in the same journey through the building. A closed partner door changes the use of an entire district. The two ends belong to one work. |
| Circle of asylum | Independently vowed houses keep a great Hallowed circuit, with interval refuges, duty paths and places of delivery outside. | Enemies may come to negotiate at its gates. Harmful work moves to the edge, where its burden falls on actual neighbors. A gap in upkeep gives visitors a reason to travel the circuit. |
| Furnace under the winter road | Core keepers and road communities maintain a banked Fire station at a cold pass. Shelter courts and supply passages grow around its limited heat. | Long-distance caravans can overwinter at altitude. Crew injury or interrupted core supply can close the route. A small settlement may exist almost entirely to keep it open. |
| Hall of the returning ships | A flotilla contributes a retired hull from each generation. Joined deck halls preserve meeting rooms, cargo scales and the original gangways of successive fleets. | The common archive can be towed to shelter or divided in a political split. An old access right can survive after its original hull has become part of the harbor wall. |
| Great meridian | Surveyors and observers maintain a sightline between an observing tower, a distant notch or marker, and a public court where observations are read. | Builders negotiate rights across a deliberately empty strip through valuable land. A missing marker gives an explorer a real bearing to follow. Multiple rulers might agree on the line while disagreeing about its use. |

## Selection and development

Use settlement scale to establish a distribution for **all** works eventually present. Most small places have none, with rare exceptions. Roughly half of mid-sized cities have one. Capitals commonly support two or three, and million-plus cities commonly support five or six. These are population-band expectations. The political title of a small seat alone does not imply capital-scale patronage.

At founding, a separate, size-weighted draw can preselect some works for larger places. Select among individual works using their own material, social and spatial requirements. A water work needs appropriate water, and a palace needs a court. Preselection records a continuing purpose and its essential site relations. A city may inherit an established institution where the parent already supplies it; a new commission can acquire its site, access and constituent premises over later rounds. Match an inherited landmark by identity to avoid founding a duplicate. Give competing works actual sites and sponsors so five works produce five places, each with its own bearing on the town.

During development, rare new works can arise when circumstances first become suitable. For example, multiple yards start sharing a fitting basin and their owners commission a fleet cradle. Tie the opportunity to that change in circumstances. Reconsidering the same basin every round as eighteen independent lottery tickets would make rare wonders commonplace. Both origins use the same POI-owned siting, construction, maintenance and publication rules. Preselected and organic works count toward the **same** scale expectation. An unusually rich history can exceed it. Avoid quotas that conjure a missing wonder in the final round or prohibit the next credible one.

## Three design cases

* **One founding template, two capitals.** Two guild commonwealths occupy comparable river estuaries. In one, shipwrights and water stewards sponsor a fleet cradle and a stair of floods. The heaviest traffic runs along the working bank. In the other, a resident court commissions a palace and surveyors protect a meridian. Workshops lose some direct river frontage to petitioners but continue behind the avenue. Neither city needs a distinct founding template for these differences.
* **A small exception.** A handful of households maintain a winter road when alternative routes become impassable. Several distant communities contribute to the furnace. The custodians and supply yard remain small; the pass has regional importance. This is an organic commission whose constituency is much larger than its resident population.
* **A metropolis with six destinations.** One plausible deep estuary supports a fleet cradle, palace, judicial island, processional lake on an inland basin, meridian and great library. A conventional library is viable without Aurora ice. The palace and prison share petitioners without sharing a front door. The shipyard and lake congregation compete for waterfront. A visitor can navigate by those relationships, and losing access to one work has a geographically specific effect.
